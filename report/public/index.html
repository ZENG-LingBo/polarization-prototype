What the pilot supports

Toxicity fell about 39% the moment the Community Note task opened — and it did so twice, in two separate sessions eleven days apart, at almost identical magnitude. It still is not a causal result. And by the time people came back, it was gone.

Replicated Toxicity drops ~0.13 when the task opens — in both cohorts −38% and −43% independently. Survives leave-one-out, the prior trend, and attrition bias.

What ranTwo cohorts, one arm

The pilot is smaller than the registration count suggests

Forty-four people registered across the two usable cohorts and fifteen ever posted. For the paired analysis — the test that respects clustering — the effective sample is the ten who wrote messages in both phases. Recruitment numbers are not sample size.

TEST1 was set up as a rehearsal, but it holds real two-session data from four returning participants. It is the only Day-2 data in the database, so it is reported here rather than discarded — with its limits stated. The other cohorts (JULY23, PRE5, AUG5, JULY22) are fragments of 2–32 messages with no phase stamps, and are excluded throughout.

The drop appears twice, at the same size

Three reasons the drop is not an artefact (checked on AUG16, where there is enough data)

  • No single person carries it. Removing any one participant leaves the drop intact, ranging +0.084 to +0.170.
  • Attrition pushes the other way. The four people who stopped posting after the free phase were less toxic than those who stayed (0.258 vs 0.364). Losing the calm participants should have raised the task-phase mean. It fell anyway.
  • It reverses a rising trend. Toxicity was climbing during the free phase (+0.0020/min). Extrapolating that trend predicts 0.422 in the task window; the observed value is 0.218.

What this can and cannot bear

Thirty-two messages from four people, one arm, no control — and the sessions were 11 days apart rather than consecutive. It cannot support a persistence claim in either direction as a confirmatory result.

What it does do is remove the assumption that the effect obviously persists. The evidence points the other way, and the honest framing is that the effect is state-dependent — present while the intervention is, gone by the time people come back. That is a contingency finding rather than a failure. It also makes the Day-2 design decision consequential rather than a formality: if the real protocol runs consecutive days, it is testing something the pilot has not tested.

Recommended reporting

Quote the effect size and the cross-cohort agreement, not a significance claim. The pilot is powered to estimate, not to test. Leading with p = 0.011 would not survive review, because the test that produces it is the one that assumes away the time confound. Two independent sessions landing within 0.002 of each other is the more persuasive fact, and it does not depend on a p-value at all.

CausalityNot identified

The control group is doing the work no test can do

In both cohorts the phase flipped at a single instant — in AUG16, last free message 11:59, first task message 12:00 — and nothing else in the study differed at that instant, because every participant was in the EXPT arm.

So “the note calmed people” and “an hour of arguing had passed and people were tiring” predict exactly the same data. No amount of re-analysis separates them. The design already solves this: the control arm sits through the same clock time with a matched-salience poll instead of the note. That cohort has never been run.

The replication across two sessions, the rising pre-trend, and the direction of attrition all argue against pure fatigue, and all belong in the write-up. None of them substitutes for the control group — and fatigue explains the Day-2 rebound perfectly well too, since people arrive fresh and get heated again. The persistence result does not break the tie either.

The framing this supports: compliance without persuasion

The intervention changed what people did without changing what they thought. That is a defensible and publishable CHI finding, and a stronger one than an attitude-change claim the data contradicts.

It also sits naturally with the Day-2 rebound: a behavioural effect with no attitudinal footing is exactly the kind that should decay once the intervention is removed. The two results tell one story rather than two. It does mean the mechanism is unlikely to be the superordinate-identity route currently in the theory section — worth reconciling before the next draft.

One methodological caution, recorded on purpose

Choosing where to measure after seeing the results — testing checkpoints until a difference appears, or falling back to “end of Day 1” if Day 2 shows nothing — is ordinary exploratory practice, and stops being defensible the moment it is written up as confirmatory. CHI reviewers ask about this.

The workable version: fix the checkpoints and the primary contrast in writing before the next cohort runs, then label everything else exploratory. Naming an expected pattern in advance is a prediction and pre-registering it is exactly right. It only becomes a problem if the cut points move afterwards to fit what came back.

MeasurementCosting power

The scorer only has eight settings

Across both cohorts the toxicity scale returns just ten distinct values on Day 1, and nearly half of all messages land on exactly 0.30. A measure that compresses this hard has less room to show a difference — some of the missing significance is instrument, not absent effect.

Before the next runActionable

Ordered by what each one buys

  1. Run a CTRL cohort Without it there is no causal claim, no matter how many participants are added. Highest-value change by a wide margin.
  2. Run a real Day 2, on consecutive days The only Day-2 data has an 11-day gap and four participants. If the protocol says two days, test two days — the pilot has not.
  3. Fix the primary contrast and checkpoints in writing first Decide the confirmatory analysis before the data exists, so the exploratory/confirmatory line holds under review.
  4. Raise participation, not just registration 8 of 17 AUG16 registrants and 21 of 27 TEST1 registrants never posted. The effective n for the pooled paired analysis was ten.
  5. Improve scorer resolution The scale produces only 8 distinct values and 46% of messages score exactly 0.30. That compresses real variation and costs power directly.
  6. Handle participant suspicion Add a suspicion probe to the debrief so it is measured rather than inferred from stray messages, and report it.
